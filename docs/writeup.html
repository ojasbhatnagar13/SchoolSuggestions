Project writeup · DPS International Edge

A suggestion box that reads what students write

Students submit ideas anonymously. An AI reads each one against the school's own rules, throws out what breaks them, spots ideas that already exist, and passes the rest to staff. Nothing reaches the school until a person approves it.

Built by Ojas Bhatnagar Live site Accompanies the demonstration video

The problem

A paper suggestion box has three failure modes. Ideas go in and nobody hears what happened to them. The same suggestion arrives twenty times and looks like twenty ideas. And someone has to read every slip, including the joke ones, before any real work starts.

This system fixes all three without removing staff from the decision. The AI sorts and filters; people still decide what the school actually does.

How a suggestion travels

Six stages, in order. Each one can stop the suggestion, and the earlier it stops, the less it costs.

A student writes a suggestion

No login, no name, no email. Nothing recorded identifies who submitted it.

Rate limit check

Runs first, deliberately — a flood is rejected before it costs a single AI request.

The AI reads it against the school rules

Returns a spam verdict, a feasibility judgement, a category, its reasoning, a summary, and a short topic phrase.

Duplicate check

The topic phrase is compared against every live suggestion. A match means nothing new is written.

Automatic sorting

Spam and rule-breaking ideas are binned. Everything else waits for a person.

Staff approve, then students vote

Only approved suggestions are ever visible to students, and only those can be voted on.

What a student sees

Three outcomes, and the student is told which one happened and why.

Queued
"We should have a chess club on Fridays" — accepted, and told it will appear once staff approve it. No silent disappearance.
Already exists
"The canteen food is watery and bad" — matched to an existing suggestion about school food quality, and shown the original to vote on instead. The wording barely overlaps; it is the idea that matched.
Not added
"Give us cricket bats at recess" — refused, quoting the school rule it breaks. Staff can still see it and overturn the decision.

What the video shows

Decisions worth explaining

Suggestions are anonymous, but votes are not

Suggestions carry no author at all — there is no column to hold one. Votes do record who voted, because that is the only way to stop one person voting fifty times. The two are stored in separate tables so a vote can never be traced back to who suggested something.

The AI decides quickly; people decide finally

Spam and rule-breaking suggestions are removed without a human ever queueing them. But "removed" means marked rejected, not deleted — every one stays visible to staff and can be restored in a click. The model will occasionally be wrong, and a design where its mistakes are unrecoverable would be the wrong design.

Duplicates are matched on meaning, not wording

Comparing raw text fails: "add bike racks" and "we need more bike racks near the gym" share almost nothing. So the AI produces a short topic phrase for every suggestion — bike racks — and matching happens on that. Two students describing the same idea in different words produce the same phrase.

The rules live in the database, not the web page

Every rule that matters — who may vote, what is publicly visible, one vote per person — is enforced inside the database. Hiding a button in the browser is not security; anyone can reopen it. These checks hold even against someone deliberately bypassing the page.

Limits, stated plainly

  • The AI is not always right. It occasionally misjudges a suggestion. That is why nothing is ever deleted and staff can overturn every automatic decision.
  • Spam protection is a deterrent, not a wall. Submission limits can be worked around by a determined person. They stop ordinary flooding, which is what actually happens in practice.
  • Voting depends on school accounts. Until the app is approved for the school's Google Workspace, students cannot sign in to vote. Submitting works regardless.

What could come next

  • A summary for staff showing which categories students raise most often
  • Email alerts when suggestions are waiting for review
  • Filtering and search once there are enough suggestions to need it
  • Archiving by term, so each year starts fresh without losing the record
  • Letting staff post a short reply when a suggestion is actioned

To try it

Submitting a suggestion needs no account — visit the site and write one. The AI's response arrives in a couple of seconds. Voting and the staff page require a school Google account, which is pending approval from the school's IT administrator.
